## [en] World news

Let's start with telecom, then move to a big shake-up in the technology industry.

Ericsson first. Ericsson and Verizon have announced a partnership on AI-native autonomous networks. The idea is to build artificial intelligence directly into the radio access network, so that the network can tune itself, detect faults and fix some of them without a human engineer stepping in. For Verizon this is about running a huge network at lower cost. For Ericsson it is another sign that the company's new chief executive, Per Narvinger, wants AI to be at the center of the sales pitch to operators. Ericsson also said its Intelligent Core now supports declarative, GitOps-style management. In plain terms, operators can describe the state they want in software code, and the core network configures itself to match, which is how cloud engineers already manage their systems.

Now the satellite side of the business. The American regulator, the FCC, has accepted a filing from SpaceX for eight hundred megahertz of spectrum for its Starlink Mobile service. Acceptance is not approval. Comments are due on the first of December and oppositions on the eighth. The filing matters because it points to a Starlink that wants to look more and more like a full mobile operator, rather than a backup for dead zones. The same week, the chip module maker Fibocom reported that it tested two ordinary low-cost LTE modules on Starlink's direct-to-cell network in New Zealand. They connected without special satellite hardware and without changed firmware. If that holds up in real products, it opens the door to cheap connected devices that work anywhere there is sky.

On the regulation side, the FCC will vote on the nineteenth of November on a plan to stop China Mobile, China Telecom and China Unicom from connecting directly to American phone numbers. The plan would also extend to other internet-based voice providers that use equipment from companies on the national security list, including Huawei and ZTE. Officials link it to the Salt Typhoon hacking campaign and to scam robocalls. China denies wrongdoing.

Now to the story that shook Silicon Valley. Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang resigned on Thursday after thirty-three years leading the company he built. His exit follows media reports about his personal relationships, including one with a woman accused of passing information to Beijing, and the board had been looking into the matter. Chief financial officer Colette Kress becomes interim chief executive while keeping her finance role, and the board has started a search that will consider inside and outside candidates. Nvidia reports earnings on Tuesday with no permanent leader in place, and investors will be listening closely for any change in the company's plans for chips and for its China business. The news arrived after the closing bell in New York, and the shares fell more than eight percent in after-hours trading.

That came after a strong day on Wall Street on Wednesday. The S and P five hundred rose about one point three percent to seven thousand six hundred nineteen. The Nasdaq gained about one point six percent. The rally was led by CoreWeave, which reported better results than analysts expected, and the S and P is now within three percent of the record it set last month.

On Iran, President Trump wrote on Thursday that the United States does not need a deal with Tehran, and told Vice President Vance to focus on domestic issues instead of the negotiations. Brent crude jumped briefly to around ninety-four dollars a barrel, then gave back part of the gain. It had fallen about a quarter in ten days on hopes of a settlement. The American naval blockade of Iranian ports stays in place, which keeps Iranian oil off the market.

In Ukraine, the three-way talks in Abu Dhabi between American, Ukrainian and Russian delegations ended without a breakthrough on the core issues, though Reuters reports that Russia and Ukraine agreed in principle on a prisoner exchange, with the numbers due Thursday. The American envoys, Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, then flew to Moscow and met President Putin at the Kremlin. Kushner said both sides agree on many principles but remain apart on how to reach peace. Overnight, Ukraine's air force said Russia fired missiles and one hundred twenty-three drones. Separately, the European Union is preparing new sanctions on Russia covering one hundred fifty more entities and fifty banks, according to French Europe minister Benjamin Haddad.

In the Middle East, Iran denies it is in talks with Washington and says the Strait of Hormuz will stay shut until its demands are met. That sits uneasily with the American claim of progress, and it explains why oil traders are so jumpy. In Gaza, an Israeli strike hit a building at Nasser Hospital in the south of the territory, and a surgeon is reported among the dead.

Back to technology and the courts. A group of nonprofits has filed a lawsuit in San Francisco against OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Google and SpaceX's AI division. They are asking a judge to halt development of the most advanced AI models, and they cite public comments by company leaders about the risks. Such a request is unprecedented, and legal experts see it as a long shot, but it shows how the debate over frontier AI is moving from conferences into courtrooms. On the money side, CNBC reports that OpenAI has raised fifteen billion dollars at a valuation of about one point two five trillion dollars. OpenAI had not confirmed the figure on its own news page when the report came out.

Sport now. In the NBA, the Los Angeles Lakers beat the Milwaukee Bucks one hundred twenty-three to one hundred twelve. Luka Doncic scored thirty-two points as the Lakers won a seventh game in a row, while the Bucks lost their eighth straight and sit at one win and eleven losses.

And the Houston Rockets won at one hundred fifteen to one hundred eight against Golden State, ending the Warriors' eight-game winning streak. Stephen Curry left that game early with abdominal soreness.

## [fr] L'actualité de la France

Voici l'essentiel de l'actualité française de ce vendredi.

D'abord le budget de la Sécurité sociale. Le Sénat a adopté jeudi l'article sept du projet de loi de financement de la Sécurité sociale pour deux mille vingt-sept, celui qui touche notamment aux allègements de cotisations patronales. Il s'agit d'une première lecture, donc rien n'est encore applicable. Les sénateurs ont déposé plus de mille deux cents amendements et siègent jusqu'au dix-sept novembre, avec un vote solennel prévu le dix-huit. Il faudra ensuite une commission mixte paritaire pour tenter de rapprocher les versions des deux chambres.

Sur le front diplomatique, le ministre délégué chargé de l'Europe, Benjamin Haddad, a annoncé que l'Union européenne s'apprête à prendre de nouvelles sanctions contre la Russie, visant cent cinquante entités supplémentaires et cinquante banques. Pour la France, le message est clair : la pression économique reste l'outil principal, pendant que les négociations d'Abou Dabi n'ont pas produit de percée.

Dans les lycées, le gouvernement hausse le ton. Jeudi, il a demandé une saisine systématique de la justice pour les actes délictueux commis dans ou autour des établissements. À Montpellier, des départs de feu ont visé deux lycées, dont le lycée Jean-Mermoz. Un mineur a été interpellé, puis rapidement mis hors de cause, et le parquet est saisi. Le ministre de l'Éducation, Édouard Geffray, promet une sévérité exemplaire. Il fait état de deux cent quarante-cinq personnels blessés depuis le début du mouvement. Le Premier ministre Sébastien Lecornu a dit soupçonner que certains incidents n'auraient pas pu avoir lieu sans des complicités au sein de l'Éducation nationale, tout en parlant d'une infime minorité. Cette déclaration fait débat. De nouveaux appels à manifester sont lancés pour samedi quatorze et mardi dix-sept novembre.
